A client accepts a fee proposal. A few months later, we’re preparing a progress claim and trying to answer a simple question. How much of the agreed fee for working drawings have we already claimed?

To answer it, we might need the original proposal, the project budget, the team’s timesheets and a copy of the last claim. Each has a purpose. The time goes into bringing them together and checking that they’re all referring to the same scope and stage of work.

That’s the kind of problem we’ve been working on at AD Architecture with **Flow Practice Manager**. We started with the way we calculate fees and put together proposals. It’s grown into a tool for following the project from the first estimate through to reviewing the work, preparing claims and getting an invoice draft ready for Xero.

## Give the project a starting point

We often price architectural work in stages. A client might engage us for design first, with working drawings and consent support to follow. The scope can vary considerably from one job to the next, even when the stages sound the same.

In FPM, we can estimate fees by stage and build a proposal from our own templates, service items and wording. We can also look back through previous quotes when we need to see how we described or priced similar work.

Once approval has been recorded and reviewed, the proposal can be used to set up the project and its tasks, with the agreed fees and starting budgets carried through. That gives the team something to refer back to when the scope changes or a stage takes more time than we expected.

## See how the job is tracking while there’s time to act

A timesheet tells us what’s already happened. At a project review, we also need to know what’s left to do.

FPM brings recorded hours, stage budgets and forecasts of remaining hours into the same review. If working drawings are getting close to budget and there’s still a substantial amount of coordination ahead, we can record the next action, who’s responsible and when we’ll check in again.

The reporting tools let us look at that from different angles. We can review one project in detail, pull together the jobs a staff member is responsible for or has worked on, or save a report that picks up stages exceeding their hours budget across the practice. We can also include imported WorkflowMax history, which matters when a job was already underway before we began using FPM.

## Carry the fee record through to billing

There’s a practical difference between the fee we agreed, the amount we’ve already claimed and what we’re preparing to claim this month. FPM brings those figures into a project financial view alongside recorded hours, costs and invoice draft status.

For a job already underway, earlier claims can be brought in as opening balances after review. For a new claim, FPM uses the stage fee schedule and previous claims to work out the amount for the current period. The project can use fees from an approved proposal or a fee schedule entered directly, which is useful for older jobs and smaller commissions agreed by email.

A reviewed claim can then feed a draft invoice for transfer to Xero. We still finalise and issue the invoice in Xero, where we manage payments and the accounts. That gives us a clear point to check that the project record and the invoice agree.

## Make past consent responses easier to find

Some of the most useful information we have is buried in old project folders. When a council asks for further information, there’s a good chance we’ve dealt with a similar question before. Finding the response is one thing. Finding the question, the project it came from and enough context to judge whether it applies is more valuable.

FPM’s RFI workspace lets us capture questions and responses from project documents, review them and search across projects. A previous response gives us a starting point for the current job, and the original context helps us judge what needs to change.

## What we’re building towards

In a small practice, the same people are often writing proposals, designing buildings, answering council questions and reviewing fees. That’s certainly true for us. When a job changes hands, we want the next person to be able to see what was agreed, what’s happened since and what needs doing next.

Flow Practice Manager is still in development, and we’re using our own projects to work out where those connections make a difference. The test is whether we can sit down for a project review, see where the job stands and decide what to do next without spending the first half of the meeting piecing the record together.
